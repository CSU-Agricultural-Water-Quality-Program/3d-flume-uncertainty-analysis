Additive Manufacturing for Irrigation Measurement: Field-scale 3D Printed Flumes for Accurate Open-Channel Flow Monitoring HighlightsLarge 60° V-notch trapezoidal flume fabricated using large-format Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) additive manufacturing Hydraulic performance validated against an industry -standard fiberglass control and theoretical rating curve Hierarchical Bayesian analysis indicated no statistical difference between the 3D printed and standard flume dischargeField traials verifiedy the structural durability and ease of installation for agricultural water monitoring applicationsAbstract.   Accurate open channel flow measurements are critical for agricultural water management, yet traditional fiberglass and metal flumes present significant financial and logistical challenges for scalable implementation. This study evaluated the hydraulic, structural, and economic viability of a full-scale, parametrically designed, large 60° V-notch trapezoidal flume fabricated with a large volume, Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) 3D printer. A laboratory comparison flow experiment coupled with a hierarchical Bayesian stage-discharge model indicated no statistical difference in the hydraulic discharge characteristics between printed flumes and industry standard fiberglass. Field deployment of the printed flume in a furrow irrigated field, capturing five irrigation events, demonstrated the structural durability and dimensional stability (±2 mm tolerance) of the print material over two months of typical field conditions. Additive manufacturing reduced costs significantly over commercial flume units, allowing for rapid fabrication and scaling in applied settings. These findings demonstrate large-scale FDM as a highly accurate, adaptable, and cost-effective method for scaling small to medium-scale water monitoring infrastructure.Keywords. Additive manufacturing, large format 3D printing, Hydraulic calibration, Irrigation monitoring, Hierarchical Bayesian analysis, FlumeAccurate measurement of open-channel water flow is a foundational requirement for irrigation management, water accounting, and water-quality monitoring across agricultural and rural landscapes. In the Western United States, where water rights determine who may divert, store, or use water at any given time and place, precise flow measurement is especially critical for ensuring legal compliance, equitable distribution, and defensible record-keeping. In the early twentieth century, Ralph Parshall, working jointly with the Colorado State University (CSU) Agricultural Experiment Station and the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Irrigation Investigations Unit, developed and calibrated what became one of the most widely recognized flow-measurement devices in the world, the eponymous Parshall flume (Parshall, 1921). For more than a century, flumes have functioned as reliable engineered structures for open-channel flow measurement in canals, ditches, and laterals. Their widespread adoption reflects not only their accessibility, but also their ability to provide consistent, predictable flow measurements across a wide range of operating conditions.Despite their proven performance, flumes produced using traditional material such as fiberglass, steel, concrete, or aluminum, present economic and practical barriers to widespread deployment for field-scale and small watershed monitoring (Harmel et al., 2023). Commercially produced flumes sized for flows of approximately 3 cubic feet per second (CFS), a common capacity for irrigation laterals in the Western U.S., typically cost ($1,800 to more than $3,000 per unit), depending on materials and manufacturer. Additionally, their bulk size and weight incur significant shipping costs and introduce lead time constraints, especially for producers, conservation districts, and irrigation companies operating in remote areas. Alternatively, flumes are often constructed locally using concrete, which can present limitations in areas where site access, materials, and/or expertise are unavailable. Additionally, concrete flumes, although relatively affordable compared to fiberglass and metal flumes, are unable to be moved from one location to another, creating an additional barrier to scalable deployment. These financial and logistical constraints can limit broader adoption of flume infrastructure needed to support accurate accounting, equitable distribution, and more efficient water management. Locally manufactured alternatives that preserve hydraulic accuracy while reducing production costs could dramatically expand access to measurement instruments essential for water stewardship across the U.S.Additive manufacturing, more commonly known as 3D printing, offers a compelling pathway toward achieving this goal. Laboratory-scale studies demonstrate that printed flumes can be produced at very low cost while reproducing the internal geometries and accurate discharge relationships required for open-channel flow measurement (Akhter et al., 2021; Oertel and Shen, 2022). Akhter et al., (2021), for example, evaluated 0.122-m HS and 0.102-m Palmer–Bowlus flumes printed in polylactic acid (PLA) and polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG), showing remarkably consistent performance across materials and replications, with adjusted R² values exceeding 97% for all tested configurations. These results established the feasibility of 3D printing to reduce costs and enable rapid prototyping of small flumes for hydrologic monitoring. However, previous work has been limited to laboratory-scale devices printed on desktop-class printers (e.g.,256 mm by 256 mm by 256 mm), leaving an important gap in understanding whether additive manufacturing can be extended to monolithically-printed field-scale, field-ready flumes that match the performance, durability, and dimensional accuracy required for agricultural water management.Current large format fused deposition modeling (FDM) printers now enable production of flumes exceeding 0.8 m in width and 1.0 m in height, raising the possibility of fabricating flumes at the scales used in irrigation ditches and laterals. Local 3D printing fabrication has the potential to reduce production costs significantly, eliminate shipping barriers, and allowing for rapid design customization through parametric (i.e., a design technique that involves creating 3D models using parameters, relationships, and constraints.) computer aided design (CAD) modeling.Therefore, the goal of this study is to create field-scale 3D printed flumes using a large FDM printer and verify flume accuracy against commercial equivalents and to demonstrate the deployment of a 3D-printed flume in-situ in conjunction with an edge-of-field water quality monitoring apparatus. This work advances both the scientific and practical foundations of additive manufacturing by applying it to agricultural water management, thereby expanding access to accurate, accessible tools that strengthen water stewardship. Materials and MethodsFlume Design and Digital Modeling  A 3D CAD model of a large 60 V-trapezoidal flume was created using Autodesk Fusion 360 (Autodesk, 2024). This flume model was selected because it is heavily utilized in the Agricultural Water Quality Program’s current research, providing accurate flow measurement capability in furrow irrigated systems around the state of Colorado. The design was based on dimension specifications from the Open Channel Flow Measurement Handbook (Teledyne ISCO, 2023) with the original design principles established by Robinson et al., (1960). A fully parametric modeling approach was utilized, where the physical geometry was constrained by defined mathematical variables and relational constraints rather than static dimensions. This allows for easy dimensional adjustments while maintaining critical geometric relationships as defined in the master V-trapezoidal flume schematic (Figure 1). Unlike fiberglass fabrications, which use static, expensive molds that lock designs in a set dimension, the parametric workflow allowed for the rapid modification of key geometric variables, such as the throat width (b2), wall thickness, and sidewall slope (𝜃), to meet site specific needs without additional re-tooling cost. A primary advantage of digitally modeled and 3D printed trapezoidal flumes is the ability to prescribe the contraction and throat geometry (key metrics defined in Figure 1 as 𝐿, 𝑏2, 𝑊, 𝐹 𝐷, and 𝜃) to intentionally force critical flow at the control section./Figure 1. Master drawing of the large 60° V-notch trapezoidal flume from Open Channel Flow Handbook (Teledyne ISCO, 2023) as originally proposed by Robinson et al., (1960) used to create the CAD design for the 3D printed model.The flume was designed as five separate components composed of a monolithic main body, a vertical support panel (acting as a leg), and three horizontal cross struts (Figure 2). The support panel and struts were printed separately to eliminate the use of 3D print supports (i.e., auto-generated structures to support the 3D printed models when overhangs exist in the printing orientation), reducing material uses and printing time. The flume body was modeled with a uniform 12 mm wall thickness and edges not in contact with the flow path were filleted (two mm) to reduce stress concentrations and print failure. A stilling well port was integrated into the design to accommodate a standard PVC pipe for water level instrumentation. Additionally, the screw holes to mount the horizontal cross struts and vertical supports panels were designed to align the parts while creating a stable structure. /Figure 2. Fabrications were performed using an Elegoo OrangeStorm Giga FDM printer (Elegoo, Shenzhen, China (~$2,500)). This system features an 800 x 800 x 1000 mm (LxWxH) build volume, which allowed the long-throated sections of the flumes to be printed as a single part, minimizing joints that could disrupt hydraulic flow. Two flumes were printed to have the ability to compare printing consistency and subsequent hydrological property impacts. The flumes were printed using a standard 0.6 mm brass nozzle with parameters optimized for structural integrity and watertight performance. PETG filament (SUNLU, Zhuhai, China) was selected as the printing material. PETG was chosen for its dimensional stability, waterproof, resistance to ultraviolet (UV) degradation, and high heat resistance without the need of a printer enclosure.  A brim was applied to the base of the print to ensure bed adhesion and mitigate warping. These prints were conducted without an enclosure. Complete printer settings are shown in Table 1.To print each flume, the main body was oriented vertically to utilize the full Z-axis height (1000 mm) of the printer (Figure 3). After the main body was completed, it was removed from the printer build plate, then cross struts and vertical support panel were printed separately. The total material used for this complete flume assembly was approximately 6.34 kg of filament (material cost ~$90) and took approximately 78 hours to print. /Figure 3. Additionally, all R code used to reproduce these results can be found in Supplementary Material S3.Field Installation and Applied Case StudyA field trial was conducted at the Colorado State University Agricultural Research, Development and Education Center (ARDEC) in Fort Collins, CO, to evaluate structural durability and performance of one 3D printed flume. The unit was installed in a furrow irrigated, 3-hectare corn field near the end of the field in an edge-of-field monitoring configuration (Figure 5). The flume was installed in an irrigated furrow with the flume floor flush with the upstream and downstream furrow ground to allow free flow of irrigation water through the flume. To prevent shifting during flow events, the flume was secured with soil and compacted using a hand tamper to lock the unit in place. To prevent flow bypass, soil berms were constructed on the upstream right and left side of the flume into adjacent furrow banks.  A CSU-developed low-cost water sampler  (LCS; Deleon et al., 2025) was deployed alongside the 3D printed flume for continuous water quality sampling and monitoring during deployment. A Milone eTape fluid level sensor (Sewell, NJ, USA) was mounted within the attached PVC stilling well, allowing for water level measurement away from surface turbulence and debris. This level data, combined with the calibrated rating curve, allowed for calculation of total flow volume. Water samples were collected from the outflow section of the flume to ensure minimal disturbance to the hydraulic profile within the throat. The trial period was from August 4, 2025, to October 2, 2025, capturing five distinct irrigation events.Following the conclusion of the field trial, the flume was uninstalled and inspected for damages and overall condition; critical dimensions, including throat width, and sidewall slope, were assessed to determine if hydraulic forces of environmental exposure had caused any deformations from the original geometry. /Figure 5. 3D printed 60° V-notch trapezoidal flume in a furrow irrigated field at the Agricultural Research Development and Education Center (ARDEC). Parameter results and uncertainties are reported in Table 4. A 95% HPDI represents the range of parameter values that contains 95% of the posterior probability mass, given the observed data and model assumptions. In practical terms, this means that, conditional on the data collected during flume calibration and the specified model, there is a 95% probability that the true parameter value, such as a rating-curve coefficient in 𝑄=𝑎ℎ𝑏, lies within this interval. This interpretation follows directly from the Bayesian framework, where uncertainty is expressed as probability over unknown quantities.By contrast, a 95% confidence interval is defined in terms of repeated sampling. Specifically, if the same experiment, such as the stage–discharge testing described in this study, were repeated many times, 95% of the constructed intervals would contain the true parameter value. However, for any single dataset, the confidence interval does not permit a direct probabilistic statement about the parameter itself. The parameter is treated as fixed, and the interval is random, which makes interpretation less intuitive in applied settings.Table 4. The fiberglass flume exhibits a steeper posterior median curvature parameter and wider posterior intervals relative to the printed units. Similarly, while the posterior scale parameters (a) for the printed flumes cluster tightly around the expected magnitude, the fiberglass flume shows greater dispersion. However, this greater uncertainty should be interpreted in light of the smaller calibration dataset (n = 4) available for the fiberglass flume compared to each 3D printed flume (n = 6). In a Bayesian framework, posterior distribution dispersion naturally reflects both data variability and information content; with fewer observations spanning the stage range, the model has less constraint on the curvature and scale parameters. Consequently, wider HPDIs for the fiberglass unit are expected under limited sample support and do not necessarily imply inferior hydraulic performance. With additional calibration points for the fiberglass flume, one would expect its posterior intervals to contract, potentially reducing apparent differences in curvature.A principal advantage of the Bayesian framework in this context is its explicit characterization of uncertainty in both parameters and predicted discharge. Rather than treating discharge observations as error-free, the model separates measurement error from structural hydraulic variability. Specifically, observed discharge was modeled as arising from a latent “true” discharge variable, with proportional measurement error and additive process variance estimated directly from the data. The use of latent true discharge variables further enhances interpretation of Figure 6, where filled points represent posterior estimates of the underlying hydraulic discharge after accounting for measurement noise. These points are what the parameters were calibrated to: not the raw data. This distinction allows separation of instrument variability from physical rating-curve behavior, which is not possible in a simple least-squares framework.This approach was particularly well suited to the relatively small number of calibration points per flume (n = 4–6). Classical nonlinear regression quantifies parameter uncertainty using asymptotic normal theory, which assumes that parameter estimates approach a bell-shaped distribution as sample size increases (Seber and Wild, 1989). Such asymptotic results are approximations whose accuracy depends on sufficient sample size. When data are limited, these normal approximations may not hold, and linearized standard errors derived from them can misrepresent true uncertainty or produce unstable coefficient estimates. In contrast, Bayesian inference does not rely on asymptotic normality for uncertainty quantification. Instead, it derives full posterior distributions directly from the likelihood and prior, allowing parameter uncertainty, measurement error, and process variability to be jointly propagated through the rating curve even when data are sparse (Gelman et al., 2013). This distinction is particularly valuable for laboratory flume calibration studies, where collecting large numbers of stage–discharge observations may be impractical and where overconfidence in standard errors could lead to misleading discharge estimates.These results demonstrate that the Bayesian model not only provided stable parameter estimates under limited data but also delivered a mechanistic decomposition of uncertainty into measurement error, process variability, and structural curvature differences. These findings align with prior work demonstrating that properly fabricated 3D printed flumes can reproduce discharge relationships with high consistency and minimal between-print variability, while highlighting the additional inferential clarity gained through probabilistic modeling (Akhter et al., 2021).Beyond parameter estimation, the practical utility of the Bayesian framework is demonstrated through the generative model aspect of the approach by creating stage–discharge predictions summarized in . Results translate the calibrated rating curves into directly usable discharge estimates across the operational depth range, with full uncertainty propagation. This is particularly important for applied water-measurement contexts, where decisions are often made at specific stage thresholds. For example, at h = 0.5 ft, both 3D printed flumes produce median predictions within 0.06 CFS of the manufacturer equation, while the fiberglass flume exhibits lower median discharge and wider uncertainty bounds. In applied irrigation and compliance settings, therefore provides a probabilistic discharge lookup framework that is more informative than a single power-law curve, enabling practitioners to quantify both expected flow and associated uncertainty at any stage within the tested range.Table 5. This reinforces that accurate stage–discharge relationships are not only important for hydrologic measurement, but are foundational to reliable water quality load estimationFuture WorkWhile this current study establishes the short-term feasibility of the 3D printed flume in field applications, future studies must focus on the long-term viability of these flumes. Although PETG material is known to have natural UV resistance, alternative materials, such as acrylonitrile styrene acrylate (ASA), that are known to have greater UV and impact resistance are ideal for long-term outdoor exposure. Future work will examine how discharge coefficients can be estimated or parameterized for field-customized flume geometries, enabling predictive application of critical-depth flumes without requiring full empirical recalibration for each new design. Furthermore, long term environmental degradation of these 3D printed flumes raises concern regarding potential dispersion of microplastics into our water system. Future work will combine standard agricultural nutrient water quality analysis to include the quantification of potential microplastics downstream of the flume. ConclusionsThe results of this study establish large-scale, 3D printed trapezoidal flumes as an accurate and cost-effective tool for agricultural flow monitoring. Laboratory calibration determined that the 3D printed flumes are hydraulicly similar to the published stage-discharge relationship for the chosen flume design, such that the 3D flumes should be considered as a reasonable replacement when compared to a commercial fiberglass equivalent. This was investigated using a novel hierarchical Bayesian parameterization of the stage-discharge model, which quantified both parameter and measurement uncertainty simultaneously. Field deployment of the printed flume supports the structural durability of the PETG polymer for this use case; the printed flume reliably functioned as designed over a two-month deployment in a furrow irrigated system, successfully weathering environmental conditions. The flume was successfully integrated with an LCS for continuous edge-of-field water quantity and quality monitoring, supporting its use in agricultural and environmental applications. Ultimately, this research provides a defensible methodology for future flume development and in situ applications that reduce fabrication costs and eliminate manufacturing lead times for scalable and accessible water flow measurement.Acknowledgments The authors would like to thank the staff at the CSU Agricultural Research, Development, and Education Center (ARDEC) and the CSU Agricultural Experiment Station for their operational support during field testing of the 3D printed flume.
